Electric Vehicle Assembly Line

Project Overview

This project consisted of the design and validation of an automated assembly line for Giant Motors Latinoamérica (GML) for the production of the JAC E10X electric vehicle. Developed over a 15-week engineering project, the objective was to design a manufacturing system capable of producing initially 20 vehicles per production shift, with future scalability toward 30 and 40 units. The scope concentrated on three major assembly areas: Station 50 for the powertrain and axles, Station 60 for battery and wheel assembly, and Station 70 for high-voltage controllers.

System Requirements & Manufacturing Constraints

The solution had to comply with industrial constraints including a 56 × 24 m available production area, defined operating times, safety requirements, existing factory infrastructure, and a maximum project budget of MXN 10 million. This established the project as a multidisciplinary optimization problem involving manufacturing engineering, robotics, automation, mechanical design, logistics, and economic feasibility. Multiple automation concepts were generated for each station and compared according to cycle time, cost, technical feasibility, ergonomics, maintainability, and compatibility with existing GML equipment.

Engineering Methodology

The manufacturing system was developed through an iterative engineering methodology combining benchmarking, concept generation, quantitative evaluation, simulation, and feedback from the industrial partner. This resulted in a systematic concept → evaluate → simulate → obtain feedback → redesign workflow. Decision-making methodologies included SWOT analysis, critical-path analysis, production-line balancing, heuristic balancing rules, weighted criteria, BOM analysis, and supplier benchmarking, creating a structured engineering process from initial requirements through implementation planning.

Manufacturing Automation Architecture

Several material-flow architectures were investigated, including conventional conveyors and an AGV-based flexible production system. The AGV concept allowed individual stations to operate with greater independence instead of forcing every workstation to advance simultaneously. The automation level incorporated PLCs, HMIs, industrial sensors, safety interlocks, emergency stops, conveyors, and AGV control, allowing sequence control, position detection, speed regulation, material tracking, and operator interaction. This modular architecture was designed to improve flexibility and permit future modifications to routes, stations, or product variants.

Station 50 – Powertrain and Axle Assembly

Station 50 focused on the assembly and installation of the front axle, rear axle, and motor/powertrain assemblies. Different robotic and conveyor-based configurations were studied before converging on a solution that exploited existing GML infrastructure such as the production conveyor, two-post vehicle lifts, lifting tables, and dollies. The vehicle body is transported into the workstation and elevated, while the respective powertrain subassemblies are positioned underneath the chassis using dedicated fixtures and handling equipment before fastening. Material transport, component positioning, operator tasks, and tooling were analyzed as a synchronized process rather than independent operations. Critical-path and line-balancing methods identified bottlenecks, resulting in a division of Station 50 into 50.1 for the rear axle and 50.2 for the motor/front-axle assembly, producing an overall assembly-line cycle time of approximately 26 minutes.

Station 60 – Battery and Wheel Automation

Station 60 integrated the installation of the 300 kg high-voltage battery and the four wheels. Initial concepts investigated ABB robotic arms for automated fastening, machine-vision systems, automated wheel handling, and a converted trolley acting as an AGV. Robot selection was performed from functional requirements: the battery-fastening robot required at least 1.2 m reach and sufficient payload for the fastening tool, resulting in the evaluation and selection of an ABB IRB 1600 with 1.45 m reach and 10 kg payload. A fastening tool capable of achieving the specified 120 Nm battery-bolt torque was also selected. After industrial feedback and cost evaluation, the station evolved toward a hybrid solution combining automation with operator-assisted processes, producing an estimated Station 60 cycle time of 28.22 minutes.

AGV Kinematics, Trajectory Modeling & Vision Systems

For battery transportation and alignment, an existing trolley concept was redesigned as a mobile robotic platform using differential-drive kinematics with two independently driven motors. Opposite wheel velocities enabled nearly zero-radius rotation, improving maneuverability in constrained production areas. Vehicle trajectories were modeled in MATLAB using the scaled factory layout, initial and final positions, and the geometric behavior of the differential-drive platform. The proposal additionally integrated Cognex industrial vision cameras to improve automatic alignment between the battery fixture, AGV, and vehicle chassis. Sensors for magnetic-line tracking, end positions, hydraulic mechanisms, and programmable control were incorporated into the architecture, connecting mobile robotics concepts—kinematics, path planning, localization, sensing, and control—with practical industrial material-handling applications.

Station 70 – Semi-Automated Human-Centered Assembly

Station 70 addressed installation of the vehicle's high-voltage electronic controllers. Fully robotic pick-and-place solutions using ABB robots and Cartesian manipulators were initially investigated, but reach requirements, additional infrastructure, interference risks, and implementation costs made complete automation less attractive. Based on GML feedback, the design was transformed into a semi-automated workstation centered on operator ergonomics and process efficiency. A vacuum lifter on a guided rail allows controllers weighing up to approximately 30 kg to be transferred from dedicated racks to the vehicle without requiring manual lifting. A second operator performs the fastening operation while logistics are simplified through strategically positioned buffers. This trade-off demonstrates an important manufacturing principle: the technically most automated solution is not necessarily the most appropriate industrial solution.

CAD, Fixture Design & Finite Element Analysis

Custom manufacturing fixtures were developed and validated using SolidWorks CAD and Finite Element Analysis (FEA). A dedicated battery JIG was designed to guarantee repeatable positioning during transportation, lifting, and chassis alignment. Design selection incorporated SWOT analysis, manufacturability, cost, safety, and compatibility with the AGV/trolley architecture. The battery JIG was analyzed using ASTM A36 steel under a 300 kg battery load and a 1.3 safety factor (approximately 3.83 kN). Initial FEA revealed yielding around hoist attachment holes, leading to iterative redesign that increased critical thicknesses. The final static analysis produced a maximum Von Mises stress of approximately 175.8 MPa versus a 250 MPa material yield strength, validating the modified geometry. Similar simulation-driven redesign was performed for the motor/front-axle fixture, including material comparison and load-distribution studies.

Digital Manufacturing & Simulation Validation

The complete manufacturing concept was validated digitally before physical implementation using complementary simulation environments. ABB RobotStudio was used to model robotic workcells, verify robot reach, evaluate trajectories, estimate operation times and speeds, visualize safety envelopes, and demonstrate interaction between robots, fixtures, operators, and vehicles. The integrated three-station model was also adapted into a virtual-reality demonstration for GML. At the factory level, Siemens Tecnomatix Plant Simulation was used as a discrete-event manufacturing model containing sources, assembly stations, drains, operators, transportation systems, processing times, and workstation statistics. Unlike the robot-level simulation, Tecnomatix enabled quantitative evaluation of throughput, utilization, waiting times, bottlenecks, and cycle-time modifications, turning the digital model into a decision-support tool for line optimization.

Production Optimization & Project Outcomes

The final stage combined cycle-time optimization, line balancing, economic analysis, and manufacturing feasibility. Tecnomatix simulations were used to test alternative processing times and identify stations with the strongest influence on throughput. Optimized scenarios exceeded the original target of 20 vehicles per shift, with evaluated configurations producing approximately 24–27 vehicles per shift. The complete proposed equipment budget for Stations 50, 60, and 70 was approximately MXN 2.30 million, requiring 12 operators and remaining substantially below the original MXN 10 million project constraint. Overall, the project demonstrated a complete industrial-engineering workflow involving manufacturing automation, industrial robotics, PLC/HMI architecture, AGV systems, MATLAB trajectory modeling, machine vision, SolidWorks CAD/FEA, fixture design, RobotStudio, Siemens Tecnomatix, discrete-event simulation, critical-path analysis, line balancing, BOM development, supplier benchmarking, design-for-manufacturing, ergonomics, and cost optimization, resulting in a technically feasible proposal for future implementation at GML.

Development Process & Engineering Workflow

1

Requirements Analysis & Concept Generation

Defined assembly line specifications including 56 × 24 m production area, 20 vehicle/shift target, MXN 10M budget limit, and three assembly stations (50, 60, 70). Generated multiple automation concepts for each station using SWOT analysis, critical-path analysis, and weighted evaluation criteria comparing cycle time, cost, technical feasibility, ergonomics, and compatibility with existing GML infrastructure.

2

CAD Modeling, Design, & Fixture Development

Designed full-scale assembly line in SolidWorks with robotic stations, conveyors, AGV systems, and custom manufacturing fixtures. Developed battery JIG and motor/front-axle fixture with Finite Element Analysis (FEA) validation using ASTM A36 steel and 1.3 safety factor. Modeled AGV kinematics using differential-drive platform with MATLAB trajectory modeling for factory-scale path planning and maneuverability analysis.

3

Robot Selection, Programming & Simulation

Evaluated and selected ABB IRB 1600 robot (1.45 m reach, 10 kg payload) for battery-fastening operations with 120 Nm torque capability. Programmed robotic motion sequences in RobotStudio, modeled workcells with safety envelopes, and created virtual-reality demonstration for GML. Integrated Cognex vision systems for automatic alignment between battery fixture, AGV, and vehicle chassis.

4

Production Simulation, Optimization & Validation

Built complete manufacturing model in Siemens Tecnomatix Plant Simulation as a discrete-event system including sources, stations, drains, operators, transportation, and processing times. Executed iterative simulations testing alternative cycle times and identifying throughput bottlenecks. Optimized line balancing using critical-path analysis and heuristic balancing rules, achieving 24–27 vehicles/shift (vs 20-unit target) with equipment budget of MXN 2.30 million (23% of MXN 10M limit) and 12 operators required.
